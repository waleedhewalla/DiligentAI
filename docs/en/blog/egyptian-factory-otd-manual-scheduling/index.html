Why Egyptian Factories Miss On-Time Delivery — and It Isn't the Machines

A three-day planning cycle guarantees your schedule is wrong by Tuesday. Here is what top-quartile plants do differently.

By Waleed Hewalla · 18 September 2026 · 6 min read

Ask an operations head in Cairo or 10th of Ramadan why on-time delivery (OTD) is below target, and you will hear about machine breakdowns, late raw materials and last-minute customer changes. All real. But none of them is the root cause.

The root cause is the planning cycle itself. When building a weekly schedule takes two to three days of Excel work, the schedule is already out of date the moment it is published. The first disruption breaks it, and there is no time to rebuild it until next week.

The three-day trap

  • Day 1–3: planners gather orders, capacity and material status and build the plan by hand.
  • Day 4: the plan is published — and a machine goes down.
  • Day 5–7: supervisors improvise. OTD slips. The cycle repeats.

Top-quartile manufacturers globally have moved to Advanced Planning & Scheduling (APS): software that generates a feasible, optimised schedule from real constraints in seconds, and regenerates it whenever reality changes. In MENA this shift is running three to five years behind.

What changes when the plan takes 60 seconds

When a schedule takes under a minute to produce, re-planning stops being an emergency and becomes routine. Planners stop building schedules and start managing exceptions. That is the change we measured at Star Trans: an 80% reduction in planning time within eight weeks of go-live.

Your schedule: built in 3 days, wrong by Tuesday. It doesn't have to be.

Where to start

  • Measure your current planning cycle in hours, honestly.
  • List the top five disruptions that broke last month's schedules.
  • Check whether your ERP holds routings and capacities accurately — that is the data an APS engine needs.
